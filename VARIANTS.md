# Variants — One Sentence for a Traveler from 1800

Bounty: `u-po-pu-one-sentence-for-a-traveler-from-1800-7e6162fb`
Gate: 1–200 characters, one sentence, UTF-8, not whitespace-only.
Judge criteria (in priority order): (1) explains what the internet is and does to
an 1800 reader; (2) built from 1800-known things (road, post office, library),
no post-1800 words except "internet" once as the subject label; (3) literally
true, no misleading claim; (4) every word earns its place, no filler, no
crammed feature list; (5) one image/phrase still remembered a week later.

Differentiation note: competitor titles show the "library + post office + road"
mashup is saturated. Every variant below therefore carries ONE comparison plus
ONE concrete action (a letter crossing the world at once) plus concrete 1800
geography (London → Canton) plus one memorable closing phrase — no generic
three-noun mashup without an action.

## V1 — road + library + post office, full coverage (142 chars)

> The internet is a road joining every library and post office on earth, so a letter written in London is read in Canton in the blink of an eye.

- (1) Strong: library = knowledge, post office = correspondence, action shows the function.
- (2) All 1800-known nouns; "internet" used once.
- (3) Weak spot: "every library and post office on earth" overclaims literally — not every library is on it.
- (4) Tight, no filler.
- (5) "in the blink of an eye" is a stock idiom, not distinctive.

## V2 — single comparison, road with no distance (135 chars)

> The internet is a road with no distance on it: write a letter in London, and a stranger in Canton reads it while your pen is still wet.

- (1) Good on correspondence; says nothing about knowledge/library.
- (2) Pure 1800 vocabulary; "road", "letter", "pen".
- (3) True as a comparison; "stranger" shows anyone in the world can be reached.
- (4) Very tight; the colon does the work of a whole clause.
- (5) "while your pen is still wet" — vivid, physical, memorable.

## V3 — library that answers back (127 chars)

> The internet is a library that answers back: ask a question in London, and the answer comes from Canton before your ink is dry.

- (1) Good on knowledge-seeking; says nothing about letters/people.
- (2) 1800 vocabulary throughout.
- (3) Weak spot: books in a library do not literally "answer back", so the comparison strains on a literal reading; also implies answers always come, which is not guaranteed.
- (4) Tight.
- (5) "before your ink is dry" — good, but weaker than V2's "pen still wet" image.

## V4 — post office with no horses and no sea (128 chars)

> The internet is a post office with no horses and no sea: post a letter in London, and it is read in Canton before the wax cools.

- (1) Good on correspondence only; no knowledge side.
- (2) "no horses and no sea" is exactly how an 1800 reader measures distance — strong.
- (3) True as a comparison; delivery without physical carriage is the point.
- (4) Tight.
- (5) "before the wax cools" — concrete, 1800, memorable.

## V5 — road joining library and post office, one action (140 chars after revision) — WINNER

> The internet is a road joining the library and the post office, so a letter written in London is read in Canton while your pen is still wet.

Revision note (second pass): the first draft read "joining library and post
office" (132 chars). Adding the two articles ("the library and the post
office", 140 chars) reads as natural English to a careful reader instead of
telegraphic shorthand, at a cost of 8 characters and no loss on any criterion
— the comparison, the action and the closing phrase are unchanged.

- (1) Strongest coverage: ONE comparison carries BOTH functions — library (knowledge) and post office (letters) — and the London → Canton action shows what it does, not just what it is.
- (2) Pure 1800 vocabulary; "internet" once as subject label; "road", "library", "post office", "letter", "pen" all known in 1800.
- (3) True: the internet does join the world's stores of knowledge and its correspondence into one reachable road, and a message written in London can be read in Canton essentially at once. No "every" overclaim (unlike V1) and no "answers back" stretch (unlike V3).
- (4) 140 chars, no filler; "joining the library and the post office" is one comparison, not a feature list.
- (5) "while your pen is still wet" — the single most physical, most repeatable phrase of the five; a reader remembers the wet pen a week later.

## Decision

**Winner: V5 (140 characters after revision).** Against V2 (the runner-up): V2 is punchier but
covers only correspondence; V5 keeps V2's memorable closing and adds the
knowledge side, so it wins criterion (1) — the first and most important —
without losing on (2)–(5). Against V1: same coverage, but V5 drops the
literally-false "every … on earth" and the stock idiom, winning (3) and (5).
